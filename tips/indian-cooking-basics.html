Indian Cooking for Beginners: Start Here

Food & Cooking 10 min read VibeAndThrive Team

Indian cuisine is one of the world's most diverse and flavourful food traditions, built on a foundation of aromatic spices, layered techniques, and centuries of regional variation. Despite its reputation for complexity, the fundamentals of Indian cooking are very learnable — once you understand how the spices work together and master a few key techniques, a whole world of incredible food opens up in your own kitchen.

The Essential Indian Spice Rack

  • Cumin (jeera): Warm, earthy base spice — used whole in tempering or ground in spice mixes
  • Coriander (dhania): Slightly citrusy, used ground in most curries and spice blends
  • Turmeric (haldi): Bright yellow, earthy, anti-inflammatory — a small pinch in almost everything
  • Chilli powder or fresh chillies: Adjustable heat — start small, add to taste
  • Garam masala: Warming blend of cinnamon, cardamom, cloves — added at the end of cooking
  • Mustard seeds: Essential for South Indian tempering — pop them in hot oil for nutty flavour
  • Curry leaves: Fragrant leaves from South India, added to hot oil for unmistakable aroma

Core Indian Cooking Techniques

  • Tempering (tadka/chaunk): Bloom whole spices in hot oil first — this releases fat-soluble aromatics
  • The onion base: Frying onions until golden-brown is the foundation of most North Indian gravies
  • Tomato reduction: Cook down tomatoes until oil separates — this signals a fully cooked masala
  • Dry roasting spices: Toast whole spices in a dry pan before grinding for deeper flavour
  • Pressure cooking legumes: Dal and chickpeas cook in a fraction of the time under pressure

Five Essential Dishes to Learn First

  • Tarka dal: Red or yellow lentils simmered and finished with a spiced oil tempering
  • Aloo sabzi: Simple potato dry-fry with cumin, turmeric, and coriander
  • Tomato rice: South Indian spiced rice with tomatoes and curry leaves — one pan, 20 minutes
  • Palak paneer: Spinach and fresh cheese in a creamy spiced sauce
  • Rajma: North Indian kidney bean curry — rich, deeply spiced, and filling

Tips for Cooking Indian Food at Home

  • Cook masala until oil separates: This is the key sign your curry base is properly cooked
  • Taste and adjust: Indian cooking is intuitive — season as you go rather than following rigidly
  • Don't rush the onions: Properly caramelised onions take 15–20 minutes — they're worth the wait
  • Add garam masala at the end: Heat destroys its volatile aromatics — stir in off heat or just before serving
  • Fresh herbs at serving: Cilantro, mint, and curry leaves added just before serving brighten everything

Mastering Tempering (Tadka): The Flavour Trigger

Tempering — known as tadka in Hindi or chaunk in Punjabi — is the technique of blooming whole spices and aromatics in hot oil or ghee to extract their fat-soluble flavour compounds before they touch anything else in the dish. It is one of the most transformative skills in Indian cooking, and it takes just a couple of minutes to execute. Done correctly, it creates an intensely aromatic foundation that infuses the entire dish.

  • Use medium-high heat: The oil must be genuinely hot before spices go in — test with a single mustard seed; if it sizzles and pops immediately, the oil is ready.
  • Sequence matters: Add whole spices first (mustard seeds, cumin, dried chillies), then softer aromatics (curry leaves, onion, garlic, ginger) — they have different bloom times.
  • Listen and smell: Mustard seeds pop, cumin turns a shade darker and smells nutty — trust your senses rather than a timer.
  • Don't walk away: Tempering takes 30–90 seconds; spices go from perfectly bloomed to burnt in moments, so stay at the pan.
  • Finishing tadka: On dishes like dal, a second tadka is poured on top just before serving — this is a signature move that adds freshness and aroma to the finished dish.

Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

Indian cooking has a learning curve, but most beginner frustrations come from a handful of easily corrected habits. Understanding what often goes wrong helps you avoid it and accelerates the path from uncertain novice to confident home cook. Most mistakes boil down to rushing — Indian cooking rewards patience at every stage.

  • Adding spices to cold oil: Spices need heat to release their aromatics — always heat the oil first, otherwise the dish will taste raw and flat.
  • Under-cooking the onions: A pale, softened onion is not the same as a properly caramelised one. Give it 15–20 minutes on medium heat for a rich, sweet, deep base.
  • Not cooking out the masala: The tomato-spice base needs time to cook down until oil separates from the mixture. Rushing this step leaves a raw, grainy taste in the curry.
  • Over-spicing from the start: It is easier to add more spice than to remove it. Start conservatively, taste as you go, and adjust towards the end of cooking.
  • Skipping the resting time: Like most dishes, Indian curries taste better 10–20 minutes after cooking as the flavours meld. When possible, cook slightly ahead of serving.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Indian cooking hard to learn for beginners?

Indian cooking has a reputation for being complex, but it's very learnable once you understand the logic behind it. Most dishes follow a similar pattern — temper spices, build an onion-tomato base, add protein or vegetables, finish with garam masala. Master the pattern and you can cook dozens of dishes confidently.

What are the most important Indian cooking techniques to learn first?

The two techniques that unlock the most dishes are tempering (tadka) and building the masala base. Tempering whole spices in hot oil or ghee is used in almost every Indian recipe. Building and cooking down the onion-tomato-spice base is the foundation of all North Indian curries and many South Indian dishes too.

Can you make Indian food without a lot of spices?

Yes. Many simple Indian dishes use just three or four spices — turmeric, cumin, coriander, and salt — and still taste incredible. You don't need a 20-spice collection to start. Build your spice rack gradually, starting with the essentials listed in this guide, and add more as you grow comfortable.

How do you make Indian food less spicy?

Spice heat in Indian cooking comes primarily from chilli powder and fresh chillies, both of which you control completely. Simply use less, or leave them out entirely — the dish will still be full of flavour from the other spices. Dairy (yoghurt, cream, or milk) added during cooking also mellows heat effectively if you've overdone it.

What are the best basic Indian dishes to learn first?

Start with tarka dal (spiced lentils) — it's forgiving, nutritious, and uses core techniques. Then try aloo sabzi (dry potato curry), which teaches tempering without a gravy to worry about. From there, move to a simple tomato-based curry like rajma or chana masala. These four dishes will give you a solid, practical foundation.

Indian cooking is a journey, not a destination. Each region has its own traditions, techniques, and signature flavours, and there is always something new to explore. Start with the spice basics, master tarka dal, and work outward from there. Within a few months of regular cooking, you'll develop an intuitive feel for how spices work together — and the ability to improvise confidently with what's in your pantry.
